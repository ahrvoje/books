L103: cite20†The KAM Story†www.amazon.com is a great example. It's more of a popularization with an unusually high level of math than a textbook. I mean, it's at the level that reading it significantly helps with reading the actual proof.
L104: As the book argues, the KAM theorem deserves to be much more well-known than it is. In a sense, it resolves the story of the n-body problem that began with Newton. For a while, the problem amounted to finding an analytic solution, which exists when n = 2. Famously, Poincare showed that the three body problem can't have a nice solution (i.e. it is non-integrable). This is actually the case for 'most' such systems--Poincare also proved that generic Hamiltonian systems were non-integrable.
L105: Poincare's result led many (including Fermi!) to believe the much stronger claim that generic Hamiltonian systems were ergodic. That isn't true either, and the KAM theorem describes precisely what is true about generic perturbations of an integrable system.

L188: Elliptic Curves, Modular Forms, and their L-Functions by Alvaro Lozano-Robledo comes to mind as does Visual Complex Analysis by Tristan Needham for two very different types of pleasant introductions. Less rigorous still, The Royal Road by Roger Penrose or Gödel, Escher, Bach by Douglas Hofstadter are great examples of "beyond popular science" type books that remain engaging to those with training.
L189: A lot more intense but still guides without full rigor, you have Manin's Introduction to Modern Number Theory and A Royal Road to Algebraic Geometry by Holme. Of course, Princeton's Companion is also not rigorous, as is the AMS What is a ...? column, but both should be consumed in small bites to allow for digestion.
