Book Description

In this comprehensive survey, William Hudson explores the forces responsible for bringing about the Renaissance, which he describes as the West's "transition from the medieval to the modern world." Voyages of discovery, inventions, the revival of classical learning, and the advent of science contribute to the intellectual upheavals of this creative period, which are reflected in its literature and art. Hudson focuses on the one thread of continuity which he sees as both the seed and the fruit of this exciting era: the awakening of secular humanism and the emergence of the individual. This history gives the listener a lucid, perceptive analysis of the splendid Renaissance.

Heavy Going

By ThomasBagot on Mar 04, 2013

The sentences and paragraphs are long and too wordy. I found it boring, pompous and illogical. While the contents make the book seem well structured. It is not. The examples given, unreferenced, sometimes in quotes, are often hard if not impossible to fathom. Somebody burned at the stake because of a sixth century dictum - A check using wikipedia and the person was burned for multitudinous things not related to anything in the sixth century. Thomas Aquinas - gets vilified for some sixteenth century reason but he belongs in the twelfth century. Some good guy popes - very educated - read Greek - are included as good guys - then there are some bad guys. At first, I was unaware that Hudson wrote this book in the early 1900s--his writing style is still fresh and contemporary, unburdened by antique diction or quaintness. In reading Hudson's book, I imagined what it would have been like for a lucky young person to take the European Grand Tour, seeing the sights, art, and architecture of the 14th and 15th centuries, accompanied by a knowledgeable tutor. The ideal tutor would have been Mr. Hudson, speaking in this voice, deeply acquainted with the period in many ways, and sharing his knowledge in brief but memorable summaries and vignettes. His discussions of Renaissance artists, augmented by apt quotations from Vasari but enlivened by his own very sharp insights, are particularly excellent. A great benefit of Hudson's version of the story are his vivid reminders that it was a period of change--from authoritarian structures and community psychological strictures (their repressions and comforts) to the emergence of secular thought and vigorous individual expression.

PREFACE

The purpose and plan of this little book may easily be gathered from the introductory chapter. Only a few words of preface, therefore, are needed.

As I conceive it, a history of English literature, however brief, should still be a history of English literature in fact as well as in name; and for a history something more is required than a list of authors and their books, and even than a chronologically-arranged collection of biographical sketches and critical appreciations. I, on the other hand, believe that the literature of any age is necessarily shaped and coloured by all the elements which entered into the civilisation of that age. So far as the limits of my space would allow, therefore, I have tried always to suggest the vital relationship between English literature and English life.

WILLIAM HENRY HUDSON.
