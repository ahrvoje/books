#### Description

A Sunday Times Art Book of the Year
What was the 'Renaissance'? In the nineteenth century this flowering of creativity and thought was celebrated as the birth of the modern world. Today many historians are sceptical about its very existence. Earthly Delights rekindles the Renaissance as a seismic change in European mentalities, in a panoramic history that encompasses Florence and Bruges, London and Nuremberg. Artists from northern as well as southern Europe, including Leonardo, Bosch, Bruegel and Titian, star in a captivating and beautifully illustrated narrative that sets their lives against a period of convulsive change across a continent that was finding itself as it ‘discovered’ the world.
Art critic and writer Jonathan Jones tells the story of Renaissance artists as pioneers, adventurers and ‘geniuses’, a Renaissance concept. Albrecht Dürer gazes with wonder on Aztec art in Brussels in 1520, Leonardo da Vinci tries to perfect a flying machine, Hieronymus Bosch finds inspiration in West African ivory carvings imported by the Portuguese to Antwerp. A then unknown Netherlandish painter, Pieter Bruegel, arrives in 1550s Rome just as Michelangelo is striving in the same city to raise the new St Peter’s Basilica towards heaven. From Atlantic voyages to Germanic woods, Italian palazzi to the royal castle of Prague, this was an age when people dared to experiment with the occult and dabble in utopias: to think and create new worlds.

Book Description

In this comprehensive survey, William Hudson explores the forces responsible for bringing about the Renaissance, which he describes as the West's "transition from the medieval to the modern world." Voyages of discovery, inventions, the revival of classical learning, and the advent of science contribute to the intellectual upheavals of this creative period, which are reflected in its literature and art. At first, I was unaware that Hudson wrote this book in the early 1900s--his writing style is still fresh and contemporary, unburdened by antique diction or quaintness. In reading Hudson's book, I imagined what it would have been like for a lucky young person to take the European Grand Tour, seeing the sights, art, and architecture of the 14th and 15th centuries, accompanied by a knowledgeable tutor. The ideal tutor would have been Mr. Hudson, speaking in this voice, deeply acquainted with the period in many ways, and sharing his knowledge in brief but memorable summaries and vignettes. His discussions of Renaissance artists, augmented by apt quotations from Vasari but enlivened by his own very sharp insights, are particularly excellent. A great benefit of Hudson's version of the story are his vivid reminders that it was a period of change--from authoritarian structures and community psychological strictures (their repressions and comforts) to the emergence of secular thought and vigorous individual expression.

The Story of the Renaissance

Heavy Going

By Thomasbagot on Mar 03, 2013

The sentences and paragraphs are long and too wordy. I found it boring, pompous and illogical. While the contents make the book seem well structured. It is not. The examples given, unreferenced, sometimes in quotes, are often hard if not impossible to fathom. Somebody burned at the stake because of a sixth century dictum - A check using wikipedia and the person was burned for multitudinous things not related to anything in the sixth century. Thomas Aquinas - gets vilified for some sixteenth century reason but he belongs in the twelfth century. Some good guy popes - very educated - read Greek - are included as good guys - then there are some bad guys. Trying to find out who and why even with google becomes impossible. The author proposes a spirit that effects renaissance and never really clarifies this greatly lauded generality is. Renaissance is said to take place between late fourteenth and early seventeenth century. Then a few pages later we are told the spring of the renaissance is in the thirteenth century. A few pages on and we are in the 11th century. "Humanists" are heroes. "Sacerdotal" classes are villains - then most of the heroes are members of monasteries. I am very interested in the subject but could not manage to finish this strange book.
