L62: We are celebrating World Book Day with the fantastic book recommendations in aerospace and aviation from the National Aerospace Library (NAL) readers and volunteers.
L63: Evidence in Camera : the Story of Photographic Intelligence in World War II by Constance Babington Smith. Section: NAL Hub.
L64: 
L65: "This is the story of the use of photographic intelligence in World War II. It covers the part played by photo intelligence in many of the exciting dramas such as the hunt for the Bismarck and the identification of the flying bomb at Peenemunde. It also covers her own personal and exciting experiences at the Allied Central Intelligence Unit. "
L66: 
L67: Mike Stanberry FRAeS NAL Volunteer
L68: 
L69: 
L70: Black Box Canberras by Dave Foster – NAL Section 36/37
L71: "My choice is "Black Box Canberras" by Dave Forster which I perused at NAL. The specific area it helped my research was a good overview of RAE's HIPSEOS programme in the early 1970s covering, inter alia, the Canberra trials aircraft which was used (WT308), the programme's purpose, and the equipment it trialled including its use of a Honeywell Helmet Mounted Sight. The book helped me draw together, and place in perspective, some other snippets and reports I had already managed to find.
L72: Based on this particular topic I would recommend the book to anyone researching any of the many, many other UK avionics and weapon systems it mentions. I have a firm impression that Forster's book is based on rigorous and extensive research using primary sources."
L73: David Thomas, Researcher into air combat simulation at the Royal Aircraft Establishment

L77: Shootdown – by Robert Johnson – NAL Section 4c
L78: "I spotted an older book on the shelf at the NAL on a topic I was interested in researching. Written only three years after the KAL007 shootdown in 1983, it was interesting to see that even then the conspiracy theories had still not settled. Being able to reflect on the more recent incidents like MH17 and the high-altitude balloons, not much has changed. It shows that airspace incursions are still just as political, dangerous, and contentious in the public eye."
L79: 
L80: [Anon]
L81: Peter Reese, Transforming the Skies; Pilots, Planes and Politics in British Aviation, 1919-1940. E-library & NAL Section 15d
L82: "Reese’s chapter on Trenchard’s fight for a separate, independent air force has been hugely useful to me, as it distils a great wealth of often complex material into an incisive and accessible grammar on the topic.
L83: Trenchard’s optimism in face of enormous political challenges from the Army and Navy, especially at a time when the 96% of his officers and 75% of his non-commissioned ranks had been discharged after the war, and given his relatively small budget, informed his concept of a streamlined Air Council and a small, specialist air force with only a small number of officers holding permanent commissions.
L84: This insight sets the scene for my chapter on the RAF’s plans to develop flying clubs as a civilian reserve, producing as many as 3,000 pilots per annum, economically, while creating an air-minded tax-paying population, which would support the RAF’s expansion."
L85: Peter Eveleigh MSc DIC, PhD researcher, University of Manchester

L89: Astra Castra.; experiments and adventures in the atmosphere by Hatton Turnor, NAL Section: Archive Room
L90: "At the end of last year I was privileged to be asked to conserve a copy of Astra Castra, which was published in 1865. I was fascinated by the numerous illustrations and the variety of printed words – from descriptions of early balloon flights to poems in more than one language. I find it amazing that a book was produced that was two inches thick and had 530 pages."
L91: 
L92: Beryl James, NAL Conservation Volunteer

L96: ‘Not much of an engineer’ : an autobiography by Sir Stanley Hooker. E-library & Section 20
L97: "While published thirty-nine years ago, this book retains a freshness of style which is fully understandable when one of Britain’s greatest ever engineers writes about his astonishing career.  At Rolls-Royce, Hooker was a success from the start, with him adding some thirty percent power to the Merlin engine.  After further successes, he left Rolls-Royce for the Bristol Aeroplane Company, where he masterminded the Olympus engine for the TSR 2 and, subsequently, Concorde.   Finally, he came out of retirement to sort out the troubles of the giant Bypass Ratio Engine, the RB 211, which had brought about the bankruptcy of Rolls Royce.
L98: This earned him a knighthood."
L99: This superb book, which is in the National Aerospace Library in Farnborough and in its e-library, should surely be read by all aspiring aeronautical engineers today, for it is sure to give both enjoyment and inspiration.
L100: 
L101: Peter Reese, Aviation Historian

L105: British Propeller Makers of World War I by Lt. Col. Bob Gardner. Section NAL Hub
L106: "There has been a Lang BE2 propeller in my family for as long as I can remember. My father was apprenticed at Waring and Gillows before the war and was one of those experienced wood workers from as many as 125 companies who turned their hands from pullman coach and ocean liner interiors to propeller making. Little did know when I was looking to find out the meaning of the many mysterious code letters stamped on the hubs that a five-volume work on the shelves at the NAL would make it all clear.
L107: A number of blade design drawings are also included and the photos, mainly in colour of factories, workshops, company logos and propellers from 8 to 20 feet in diameter bring the books alive."
L108: Rob Perry

L112: British combat aircraft in Latin America by Santiago Rivas. Section 36/37
L113: 
L114: "I recently enjoyed going through this large and profusely illustrated volume describing the many British-built aircraft used by Latin American armed forces since 1940. There are smaller sections on British aircraft used by Latin American Airlines. There is also coverage of the presence of British forces in Latin America, including military deployments, such as the Falklands, as well as demonstrations and visits."
L115: David Potter, NAL Volunteer

L119: The Enigma of the Aerofoil, Rival Theories in Aerodynamics, 1909-1930 by David Bloor. E-library & NAL Section 1a
L120: 
L121: "Assumptions are key to a successful theory and the choice of assumptions has a sociological dimension.  Sociologist David Bloor used the early development of aerofoil theory as a case study to explore this.  The result is both a compelling historical account and the most lucid and detailed explanation of the resulting aerodynamic theory."
L122: John Robertson MRAeS

L126: Farnborough: 100 Years of British Aviation by Peter J Cooper, NAL Section 14
L127: "A book which stands out to me is 'Farnborough: 100 Years of British Aviation' by Peter J Cooper and published by Midland in 2006 to mark the Centenary of the Royal Aircraft Establishment. This was a big subject to tackle but the author covered everything in a clear and logical manner. It is a great reference book and a great story, and today one can sometimes forget just what an extraordinary organisation the RAE used to be. It must have been fascinating to work there. Very highly recommended!"
L128: Tony Buttler, Aviation Historian & 2022 Winner of the RAeS Aeronautical Heritage Specialist Group Award

L132: North American X-15 : including the X-1, X-2 and Skyrocket by David Baker, NAL Section 36/37
L133: "My passion is reading about innovations in history and seeing how people overcame challenges and David Baker’s X-15 book captures that. When you come into the National Aerospace you become enlightened and enthralled by the journey made by those in our industry and are surrounded by the hallmarks of innovation."

L139: Fluid Dynamic Drag and Fluid Dynamic Lift by S F Hoerner. NAL Section 1a
L140: "These two books are examples of the type of material available on the shelves of the National Aerospace Library.  The books give data for the Lift and Drag forces on wing, fuselage and empennage and especially the drag due to the undercarriage, wires and struts etc. This is in the form of tables, graphs and charts. The publications are, I think, now out of print and are difficult to source.
L141: The author, a practicing aerodynamicist in pre-war Germany, gives an authoritative flavour to the books. For example he demonstrates the Drag build up on the Messerschmitt Me 109 fighter on which he worked. After the war, like many German technologists, he went to work in America and realizing that there was scarce information on the subjects looked for a publisher.  Unable to find any interest - he published the books himself."
L142: 
L143: John Murray
