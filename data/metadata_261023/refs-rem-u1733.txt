A foremost advocate for democratizing digital fabrication has been MIT Media Arts and Sciences professor Neil Gershenfeld. His almost 20-year-old book Fab: The Coming Revolution on Your Desktop described a world akin to what futurologist Alvin Toffler prophesied in his Third Wave – a world of productive consumption or “prosumption.”21 Digital fabrication machines, now desktop-sized and easier to use through the support of resource and expertise-sharing communities such as the one he set up in his class How to Make (Almost) Everything at the MIT Media Lab, would allow for personal fabrication. Personal fabrication did not only mean making objects for one’s own use, such as 3D-printing a coffee cup, but also channeling imagination and creativity; a trope nostalgic of the early years of computing and the internet. Gershenfeld also sermonized bolder visions, a machine that could make other machines, and ultimately matter itself, as programmable material capable of computation – oddly a more familiar vision in architectural digital fabrication than the rest of the book’s propositions.22 Gershenfeld’s book had a wide reach. Testament to its readership and audiences was a review by renowned post-phenomenologist philosopher Don Ihde for the major science and technology journal Nature.23 Ihde’s review scathed the ushering of yet another “revolution” and the book’s “technofantastical” tone but found one of its aspects to be “infectious” and “possibly of revolutionary importance.”24 This was the way that Gershenfeld had “exported” and “prepared for use by children,” in Ihde’s terms, a packaged piece of digital fabrication infrastructure known as a “Fab Lab.” In 2006, Gershenfeld was describing fab labs as small-room-sized places consisting of fabrication equipment that required about $30,000 to set up but was projecting a drop in the cost to $10,000 or even $1,000 once machines would become smaller and more affordable.25 In 2016, the global network of Fab Labs was counting about 1,000 facilities worldwide.26 Ihde saw the promise of fab labs as one of technology democratization and of self-learned skills through eight-year-old children playing with the machines.27 The democratization promise has dominated many accounts of fab labs, and the so-called “maker movement” that they have supported and instigated. The Fab Lab network grows together with a distributed network of learning resources, the “Fab Academy,” that offers the required training and skills.28 Like other democratizing narratives associated with digital technologies, global digital making comes with its own contentious histories and social visions.
