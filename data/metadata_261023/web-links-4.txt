# The Digital Hand Stamp: How Sneaky Hackers Steal Your Logins

SAMUEL MWANGI

### SAMUEL MWANGI

Published Aug 27, 2026

+ Follow

Imagine you go to a theme park and buy a ticket. The park ranger stamps your hand so you don't have to pull out your wallet and show your ticket every single time you want to ride a roller coaster. As long as that stamp is on your hand, the workers let you right in.

In the digital world, session hijacking happens when a sneaky attacker steals your hand stamp and uses it to get on the rides pretending to be you.

### What is a "Session"?

When you type your username and password into a website like email or social media, the website verifies who you are. Repeating that full login every time you click a new link would be annoying, so the website hands your browser a tiny piece of digital data called a session cookie (your digital hand stamp).

The cookie tells the website that you already proved who you were a minute ago. The entire period of time you stay logged in and browse around is called your active session.

### What is Session Hijacking?

Session hijacking (also called cookie stealing) happens when someone secretly copies your session cookie while your session is still active.

The dangerous part is that the attacker does not need to know your password.

Once they slip your stolen cookie into their own web browser, the website genuinely believes the hacker is you. They can read your private messages, buy things with your saved credit card, or change your account settings before you even realize what happened.

### How Attackers Steal Your Digital Hand Stamp

Hackers use a few clever tricks to snatch your session cookies:

  * Unsecured Public Wi-Fi: On free Wi-Fi at a coffee shop or airport, unencrypted data flies through the air. Attackers on the same network can use software to catch your cookie as it travels to the website.
  * Cross-Site Scripting (XSS): An attacker injects hidden, malicious code into a website. Visiting that page causes the code to silently send a copy of your session cookie straight to the hacker.
  * Malware and Spyware: A virus on a device can read your browser's internal files and steal stored session tokens directly off the hard drive.
  * Public Computers: If you use a computer at a library, hotel lobby, or school, the session cookie sits in that browser's memory. Walking away without logging out lets the next person inherit your active session instantly.

### Why Closing the Tab Is NOT Logging Out

Simply clicking the "X" to close your browser window on a public computer does not destroy your hand stamp.

  * Closing the Tab or Browser (Unsafe): The website still thinks you are nearby. The cookie stays valid in the computer's memory until it expires, which could take hours or days.
  * Clicking "Log Out" (Safe): The website immediately destroys the session key on its servers and wipes the cookie from the browser.

If you just close the window on a hotel computer, the next guest can open the browser, type in gmail.com or amazon.com, and instantly land inside your logged-in account without ever typing a password.

### How to Protect Yourself

Protecting your accounts from session hijacking comes down to a few basic habits:

  * Always Click "Log Out": On shared or public devices, explicitly click the Log Out button every single time.
  * Use Private or Incognito Mode: Opening an Incognito window ensures that closing it automatically wipes all cookies, browsing history, and temporary session files.
  * Avoid Sensitive Tasks on Public Networks: Save online banking or sensitive account management for your personal devices connected to a trusted network.
  * Look for the Padlock (HTTPS): Ensure website addresses start with https:// to encrypt your connection so network snoopers cannot read your cookies in transit.
  * Turn On Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA): Many modern security systems notice if a session cookie suddenly jumps to a different location or device and will ask for an extra verification code to block the hijacker.--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Thermal proxy- or self-administered quantitative sensory testing

Cold sensitivity was assessed with a 10-mL syringe filled with frozen water. For cold pain tolerance, participants were asked to apply and hold the long surface of the syringe on their skin 3 times until they could no longer tolerate the cold pain for up to 60 seconds. For cold pain sensitivity, participants were also asked to report the intensity of their pain on the NRS 0 to 10 after 30 seconds has elapsed. If participants were unable to tolerate the cold pain for at least 30 seconds, a pain intensity score of 10 was assigned.

Heat sensitivity was assessed with a digital hand warmer (Anbooc, purchased from Amazon.com). The hand warmer has 3 instant levels of heat (low at 95°F, medium at 114°F, and high at 122°F). Initially set at the lowest temperature level (~35°C), for heat pain tolerance, participants were asked to apply and hold the hand warmer on their skin 3 times until they could no longer tolerate the cold pain for up to 60 seconds. For heat pain sensitivity, participants were also asked to report the intensity of their pain on the NRS 0 to 10 after 30 seconds has elapsed. If participants were unable to tolerate the heat pain for at least 30 seconds, a pain intensity score of 10 was assigned. Heat pain sensitivity was also repeated with the digital hand warmer set at the medium temperature level (~45°C).
