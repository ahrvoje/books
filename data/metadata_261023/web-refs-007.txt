In an early correspondence between the oil commissioner of the Iranian government and the Iranian Chargé d’Affaires in London concerning labour recruitment in the oil industry, the terminology used to describe unskilled labour, including Ottoman subjects, encompassed both the terms ʿamaleh and ‘coolie’. Notably, in this same document, the term kargar was reserved specifically for the non-Asian workforce, which included British, Australian, Portuguese, and some Ottoman subjects.61 Over the ensuing years, the nomenclature used by both the employer and government agencies to refer to the unskilled workforce within the oil industry predominantly remained ʿamaleh, with occasional references to coolie or tandil.62 This linguistic practice persisted until the late 1920s, reflecting the distinct categorisations and perceptions of the workforce based on skill level and ethnic or national background. Such terminology not only illustrates the hierarchical structure of the workforce but also sheds light on the broader sociopolitical context in which these labour categories were embedded. In the nascent stages of the oil industry, the self-perception of the working people was primarily that of raʿiyat (subject) or bandeh-e haqir va jan-nesar-e homayuni (the slightest and most obedient servant of his majesty, the King), rather than identifying themselves through labour-specific terms like ʿamaleh or kargar. This perspective is evident in the absence of self-referential use of ʿamaleh or kargar in any petitions from the workers prior to the First World War. During this period, it was predominantly the employer and the government that utilised the term ʿamaleh to categorised certain segments within the unskilled workforce. However, a noticeable shift occurs in the post-First World War era. Some petitions from this period begin to adopt the term kargar to describe the workforce. An illustrative example of this change in self-identification is found in a 1924 petition to the national Parliament. NOTE ON TRANSLITERATION
In transliterating Persian words, the present work adheres to the guidelines set forth by the journal Iranian Studies. Hence a modern Persian pronunciation approach has been uniformly applied across the text, with two exceptions. Firstly, for geographical names, effort has been made to utilise their widely established anglicised forms. Secondly, for personal names, I use a doubled ‘s’ to render phonetically common Persianised Arabic names such as Hassan or Hossain; however, the preference of individuals for specific spellings has been respected. For instance, the variation between ‘Manuchehr’ and ‘Manouchehr’ illustrates this principle: while the former aligns with the standardised transliteration rules, the latter reflects the personal choice of the individual concerned when applicable. I do not use diacritical marks to distinguish between the Persian long and short vowels.

A significant portion of these workers were Chittagonian Sunni Muslims who had initially entered the Burmese oil industry in the 1890s.29 In the administrative records of the APOC and the British colonial archives, there was no specific categorisation based on the social, territorial, ethnic, or religious backgrounds of Indian migrant workers, a practice that was also applied to Iranian workers. As a result, all migrant workers from India in the Persian oil industry were generically classified as ‘Indians’. To encourage longer-term commitment, the company shifted to a fortnightly payment schedule. This change in the payment method was the company’s initial strategy to promote job continuity. However, this new payment rule had potential longterm implications. It risked drawing workers into indebtedness to local bazaar moneylenders. Such indebtedness, while unintended, could inadvertently bind workers to their jobs longer as they worked to pay off debts, thereby securing a more stable workforce for the company. 38 Oil Discovery & the Formation of a New Iranian Society In the initial phase of the Oil Company’s operations, labour discipline was enforced through long working days and modern time measurement. The early narratives from the Iranian workforce indicate a lack of a standardised workday. Iranian workers, along with their Indian and Ottoman counterparts (often referred to as ‘Turks’), were expected to work seven days a week, from sunrise to sunset. This demanding schedule was reflective of the intensive labour demands during the early development of the oil industry. However, as the industry evolved and particularly on the eve of the First World War, a new workday regime was introduced. The revised schedule entailed a six-day workweek, with the length of the workday varying between nine to twelve hours depending on the season.
