## Evolution of Scholarship Regarding

the China–Latin America Relationship
China’s expanding engagement with Latin America and the Caribbean
has given rise to an enormous volume of literature. Aside from journalis-
tic accounts, however, almost none of these works explicitly addresses the
new Chinese physical presence in the region, and the way that it impacts
both Latin America, and the way that the P.R.C. relates to the region.
As the most significant dimension of the new Chinese engagement
was economic, much of the early literature focused patterns of trade and
investment between the P.R.C. and Latin America and the Caribbean,

Methodology
In examining the impact of the new Chinese physical presence on Latin
America, this book takes a principally qualitative approach, focusing on
describing and performing a preliminary analysis of patterns in a large
number of cases. Because so little has been written outside Mandarin
Chinese beyond journalistic accounts explicitly regarding Chinese com-
panies on the ground in Latin America and the Caribbean, the challenges
that they face, and the way that they are impacting the region, this book
seeks to cover what is happening in as broad and comprehensive fashion
as possible, with the understanding that much remains to be done with
respect to more detailed comparative analyses43 and quantitative studies
of the phenomenon.
In general, the details contained in this book come from a combina-
tion of primary source material and expert interviews. Where appropri-
ate, secondary sources are used, yet this book is not intended to be a
theoretical treatise, but rather, a work that paints a broad picture of what
is happening and how it is both changing the region, and China’s rela-
tionship with it.

Organization
This book is organized into two parts. The first describes and identifies
the most significant characteristics of the new Chinese presence on the
ground in Latin America and the Caribbean. The second part focuses on
the dynamics and challenges that this new physical presence is creating
for Chinese companies, Chinese nationals, and Chinese ethnic commu-
nities in the region.

## ADDRESSING THE NEW CHALLENGES

Overview
The thesis of this book is that the expanding physical presence of Chinese
companies and personnel in Latin America and the Caribbean creates
new challenges for the Chinese. Some of these challenges include the
establishment of projects and operations in the region, as well as the
management of operational challenges once those companies are estab-
lished. As Chinese commercial entities confront both types of challenges,
the Chinese government will confront increasingly difficult decisions
regarding how, and under what circumstances, to use its growing soft
power to protect and advance the interests of those companies, their per-
sonnel, and the overseas Chinese community more broadly, even as their
physical presence also impacts the dynamics of the region.
The challenges faced by Chinese companies as they establish them-
selves on the ground in the region do not arise because they are inherently
less respectful of laws, labor standards, and the environment.1 Rather, the
difficulties faced by these companies are the same as those faced by any
newcomer rapidly expanding its human and corporate presence in Latin
America. Nonetheless, a conf luence of factors increases the magnitude
of the challenge for Chinese companies and the likelihood of negative
outcomes: (1) new Chinese physical presence is particularly concentrated
in sectors in which social and environmental impacts (and associated sen-
sitivities) are particularly high, including mining, petroleum, agriculture,
construction, and manufacturing; (2) the “cultural distance” between the
Chinese management of the companies and the Latin American govern-
ments, workers, and communities with whom they interact increases the
possibilities for friction, misunderstanding, and conf lict; and (3) the lack
of experience of these companies in dealing with Latin America and the
Caribbean increases the possibility that the challenges that do arise will
be managed poorly.2

CHAPTER 7

## THE STRUGGLE TO ACQUIRE COMPANIES AND

WIN PROJECT WORK IN LATIN AMERICA

Overview
As Chinese companies and personnel seek to enter Latin America and
the Caribbean, the first set of challenges that they face concerns their
attempts to acquire assets, and win and set up projects. The difficulties
that the Chinese encounter in doing so arises from all directions: obstacles
to mergers and acquisitions, objections by extraregional actors to Chinese
investments and projects, resistance from regional governments at both
the national and local level, attempts by commercial competitors and other
parts of the private sector to stop them, and objections from the political
opposition and other actors who were not included in the government-to-
government deal and any corresponding financial bonanza.
As noted in the previous chapter, such opposition will not occur sim-
ply because the companies involved are based in the P.R.C., although the
latter may play some role.
The reasons given by opponents and others for resisting the Chinese
presence are varied, but recurring themes include opposition to the
acquisition of land (particularly by Chinese, although this is seldom offi-
cially stated), objections to the use of Chinese laborers, and concern over
impacts to the environment and local communities, to name just a few.
Even projects which are not derailed over concerns such as these may still
fail to come to fruition because an arrangement cannot be reached with
the host government and local players sufficient to make the deal eco-
nomically viable, or because adequate funding cannot be arranged.

As local actors, Chinese companies confront a myriad of challenges

As noted previously, the challenge faced is, to some degree exacerbated
by a higher-than-average level of distrust toward the Chinese, while the
ability of the Chinese to manage such situations is impeded both by bar-

perhaps even its regime survival, depended on the success and well-being
of its commercial representatives in distant parts of the world.
As the P.R.C. awakens to the new strategic imperatives that confront
it to advance and protect the interests of its companies and nationals as
a key part of its pursuit of national development, the challenges that it
confronts are formidable. It must act in a context in which its actions, and
those of its companies and nationals are often viewed with suspicion and
mistrust, and in which the possibility of making f lawed decisions on all
sides is high because of the very cultural gulf that separates the P.R.C.
from the actors with whom it must deal. Moreover, in Latin America, it
must act under the nervous gaze of the United States, with the risk that,
if its actions in Latin America are perceived as a strategic threat to the
United States, the damage to the Sino-US relationship could put at risk
financial relationships and access to markets and technologies that are
even more fundamental to China’s long-term development. 32
It is impossible to anticipate completely or precisely how the grow-
ing physical presence of Chinese companies in Latin America and the
Caribbean will impact the region, its relationship with the P.R.C., and
the US. It is almost certain, however, that the resulting dynamics will
include a wealth of misperceptions and miscalculations by all parties
involved. It is unlikely that the narrative that dominates the history of
Latin America and the Caribbean in the twenty-first century will focus
on either the domination of the region by the P.R.C., or its “failure”
there. Rather, it is likely to be a story of interaction and adaptation that
transforms all of the parties involved, including not just individual actors
and Chinese companies, but also the states of the region, the P.R.C., and
the United States as well.--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
