# SHOOTDOWN: Flight 007 and the American Connection

by R.W. Johnson ‧ RELEASE DATE: June 2, 1986

An investigative tour de force that piles evidence upon evidence to demonstrate that the only viable hypothesis, given the current available information about the flight of Korean Air Lines Flight 007 that was shot down by the Soviet Union on September l, 1983, is that the airliner was on a passive espionage probe for the CIA. Johnson (politics/Oxford) does not suggest that the Soviets' trigger-happy posture was any the less heinous. But he simply destroys the American version of the story. The elements of the tale add up to a hypothesis that will be difficult to gainsay, Johnson asks us to consider the following: (1) only five weeks prior to the flight, the US had discovered a vast new radar system being built in Central Siberia, which called into question the Soviets' commitment to the SALT I Treaty; (2) The pilot of KAL 007 was the ace of their staff who had gained a reputation for aerial military maneuvering; (3) The plane, despite sophisticated guidance systems and checks and balances, began to deviate off course immediately after takeoff from Anchorage and was 365 miles off course when it was destroyed; (4) The pilot had arranged for excess fuel at Anchorage, sufficient to take him off course, and had made last-minute handwritten notations in the flight plan suggesting the actual deviation; (5) US RC-135 spy planes seemed to rendezvous with the KAL 007 during the flight; (6) A sister plane, KAL 015, flew near to the path of the 007, and there is strong evidence to suggest that 015 relayed erroneous information back to ground control to provide assurance that 007 was on course (the pilot of 015 has been in strict media quarantine since the disaster); (7) The 007 flew directly over several strategically crucial Soviet military facilities on Kamchatka and Sakhalin islands. Johnson ultimately suggests (and his conclusion is hard to refute, considering some of the actions of the US intelligence establishment recounted here) that the purpose of the flight was to force the Soviets to switch on all of their radar devices, thereby reaping an intelligence harvest for American satellites flying overhead. Ernest Volkman, editor of the journal Defence Science, has written, ""As a result of the KAL incident, US intelligence received a bonanza the likes of which they have never received in their lives. Reason: because. . .it managed to turn on just about every single Soviet electromagnetic transmission over a period of about four hours and an area of approximately 7,000 square miles. . ."" Food for thought that will require a full-fledged investigation of the Challenger shuttle variety to clear the air. ""269 pawns in their game,"" to quote Bob Dylan.

In the early hours of 1 September 1983 a Korean Airlines Boeing 747 was shot down by a Soviet fighter and plunged into the Sea of Japan with the loss of all its 269 passengers and crew. Reactions around the world were of horror and indignation, and the second cold war passed into a yet more frigid phase. The Reagan administration energetically propagated the view that KAL 007 had somehow drifted fatally off course as a result of an unforeseen human or technical error — a view which, at the time, widely accepted. As time has gone by, however, doubts have grown as to whether this explanation could possibly be true.

# Shootdown: 2flight

### R. W. Johnson. Viking Books, $18.95 (335pp) ISBN 978-0-670-81209-7

Image

When Korean Airlines flight 007 was shot down by a Soviet fighter in 1983, many questions and contradictions emerged that still haven't been answered. Johnson's absorbing study explores the complex affair from various angles and provides a broad context, including an assessment of the strategic balance in Northeast Asia, the use of civilian airlines for espionage and the way international crises are handled by the Reagan administration. Presented here are the four major theories of what happened and why: that Flight 007 strayed off course by accident; that the pilot tried to take a shortcut to save fuel; that the Soviets lured the plane off course through electronic interference with navigational equipment; and that 007 was, as the Soviets have claimed, on a surveillance mission. In the book's piece de resistance, Johnson reruns the sequence of events surrounding the flight as if hypothesis four were true, and most readers will likely agree that it is the only one that makes sense. Johnson is a Fellow in Politics at Oxford. Photos. 50,000 first printing. (June 2)

## ►Shootdown - The Verdict on KAL 077/R. W. Johnson (Chatto and Windus, £10.95)

T his excellent, terrifying book makes it quite clear that the Korean jumbo jet shot down by the Russians in September 1983 was on a surveillance mission for the American secret services. By inference it makes it equally clear that the men Ronald Reagan’s immediate circle will stop at nothing in their attempts to achieve military superiority over the USSR.

R. W. Johnson applies a remorseless logic to the sequence of events which constituted this tragic affair. He proves beyond any reasonable doubt that from the moment it took off from Alaska, the Boeing 747 was deliberately directed towards one of the Soviet Union’s most sensitive military installations with the sole purpose of enabling electronically-packed aircraft and ground stations to register and analyse the inevitable Russian radar response. Every aspect of this fatal flight is minutely explored and the evidence scrupulously artirtiassed, detail by detail, to a masterfully constructed case for the prosection, of Reagan’s advisers.

Mr Johnson is also scathing about Reagan himself: “Even the White House staff describe President Reagan, somewhat euphemistically, as having ‘a limited attention span’.” Elsewhere he writes that “probably not since Warren Harding has the US had a President who knows less about foreign policy than Ronald Reagan”. That this is the man with his finger on the button does not make for peaceful sleep. <1
