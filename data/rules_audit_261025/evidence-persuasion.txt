# Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion

The book that explains why people say yes. First published in 1984 and expanded in 2021 with a seventh principle. Dr. Cialdini’s books, led by Influence, have sold more than 7 million copies in 44 languages.

What It’s About

## Why people say yes.

Influence answers one question: what leads a person to agree to a request? Dr. Robert Cialdini, a social psychologist at Arizona State University, found that most decisions to comply follow a small set of principles. Each one is usually a sound shortcut, which is why it works, and why it can be exploited.

The book shows the research behind each principle, how it plays out in sales, marketing, management and daily life, how to use it honestly, and how to recognize and defend against it when someone uses it dishonestly.

### How it was researched

Besides decades of laboratory and field experiments, Cialdini spent years undercover. He signed up for the training programs of people who persuade for a living, among them car sales, fundraising and telemarketing, to learn which tactics consistently worked. The same few principles kept appearing beneath very different methods.

Inside the Book

## The seven principles of persuasion.

Six principles in the original; Unity was added in the New and Expanded edition. Each has a full guide here, with the research and how to use it.

  1. IReciprocityReciprocity is the rule that obliges us to repay what another person has given us.
  2. IIScarcityScarcity is the tendency to assign more value to things that are rare, dwindling or hard to obtain.
  3. IIIAuthorityAuthority is the tendency to defer to people we take to be knowledgeable or entitled to direct us.
  4. IVCommitment and ConsistencyPeople want to act in ways consistent with what they have already said and done.
  5. VLikingWe agree more readily with people we like, and the things that make us like someone are more mundane than most people expect.
  6. VISocial ProofSocial proof is the tendency to decide what is correct by finding out what other people think is correct.
  7. VIIUnityUnity is the most recently identified of the principles, and it is not a stronger form of liking.

Questions People Ask

## About the book.

What is Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion about?+

It explains why people say yes. Robert Cialdini identifies the principles that lead people to agree to a request, shows the research behind each one, and teaches how to use them ethically and how to recognize when they are being used on you.

What are the principles in Influence?+

Reciprocity, Commitment and Consistency, Social Proof, Authority, Liking and Scarcity in the original 1984 book, and Unity, added in the 2021 New and Expanded edition. That makes seven principles in the current edition.

Which edition of Influence should I read?+

The New and Expanded edition (Harper Business, 2021). It contains all seven principles, including Unity, and updates the research and examples, including online applications. Influence: Science and Practice is the academic version for instructors and students.

How was Influence researched?+

Alongside decades of laboratory and field experiments, Cialdini spent years undercover, signing up for the training programs of people who persuade for a living, including car sales, fundraising and telemarketing, to see which tactics consistently worked and why.

What is the difference between Influence and Pre-Suasion?+

Influence explains the principles that move people to say yes. Pre-Suasion (2016) is about the moment before a request: how what people are focused on just before a message changes how they respond to it. Read Influence first.

The Science of Earning Respect
Date: Thu Sep 18 10:23:39 2014 UTC

At first glance, little differentiates Berkshire Hathaway stockholder reports from those of any other major corporation. But look closer. Even in years when Berkshire has been unimaginably successful, often the first few pages of Warren Buffett's chairman's notes draw attention to a snag or strain in the company. In a reputation-obsessed world, has Buffett got it wrong? Hardly! Researchers who study persuasion know that messages can be amplified when people present a small weakness in them, which in turn garners a higher level of trust.
It would be ridiculous to suggest that the admission of a weakness will alone be enough to change the way people think of you, of course. A second strategy: arrange for someone to toot your horn on your behalf. In one study for our book, customers calling a real estate agency about selling their property were honestly told, "I'm going to put you through to Peter. He is our head of sales and has 20 years of experience selling properties in this area." The impact was immediate and impressive. We measured a nearly 20% rise in the number of face-to-face appointments and a 15% increase in the number of customers who used the agency.
And third, potential exerts a persuasive pull over reality. Facebook users who were shown a series of quotes about a comedian registered much greater interest when informed of his potential ("This guy could become the next big thing") rather than his achievements. Want to win friends and influence people? We'll let them know you're the next big thing.
